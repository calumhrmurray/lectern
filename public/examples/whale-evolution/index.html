Where whales sit on the tree of life

Whales are even-toed ungulates. Their closest living relatives are hippos.

  • Cetaceans nest inside Artiodactyla (pigs, camels, ruminants, hippos) — hence the name Cetartiodactyla.
  • Molecular evidence: shared SINE retroposon insertions found only in whales and hippos (Nikaido et al. 1999) — near-irreversible markers of common ancestry.
  • Fossil confirmation: early whales Rodhocetus and Artiocetus have the artiodactyl double-pulley astragalus (ankle bone) (Gingerich et al. 2001).
  • The whale + hippo clade is called Whippomorpha.

Before the return · leaving the water

Whales descend from fish that crawled out ~375 million years ago.

  • In the Late Devonian, lobe-finned fish related to today's lungfish and coelacanths gave rise to the first four-limbed vertebrates, the tetrapods.
  • Tiktaalik roseae (~375 Ma, Ellesmere Island): a flat-headed fish with a neck, ribs, spiracles and robust fins with wrist-like bones — it could prop itself up in the shallows (Daeschler et al. 2006).
  • Acanthostega and Ichthyostega (~365 Ma, Greenland): true limbs with eight and seven digits, yet still largely aquatic — limbs evolved in water before they were used on land (Coates & Clack 1990).
  • Trackways from Zachełmie, Poland, suggest limbed animals walking as early as ~395 Ma (Niedźwiedzki et al. 2010).

The reverse of the whale story. The fish-to-tetrapod transition moved the same equipment the other way: paired fins became weight-bearing limbs with digits; the skull separated from the shoulder to make a neck; lungs, inherited from bony-fish ancestors, took over from gills; the ear was rebuilt for airborne sound. Whales later undid much of this — and kept the lungs.

Air breathing predates land: lungs are an old bony-fish feature, still present in lungfish.

Before the return · 325 million years on land

From the Devonian shallows to the Eocene shore: the long way round.

  • Every mammal, bird and reptile alive today is a tetrapod: the ancestors of whales spent roughly 325 million years as land animals before turning back.
  • On the way they acquired what whales still carry: the amniotic egg (later, live birth), warm blood, milk, and the mammalian middle ear — three tiny bones whales rebuilt for hearing under water.

So the whale pathway is water → land → water. The return took ~10 million years; the stay on land took thirty times longer. That is why a whale's blueprint — lungs, warm blood, live birth, suckling, a few whiskers on the snout — is a land mammal's, redeployed (whales even lost their skin glands).

Dates are conventional first-appearance ages: earliest tetrapods (Devonian, ~375–365 Ma), earliest amniotes (Carboniferous, ~315 Ma, e.g. Hylonomus), earliest mammaliaforms (Late Triassic, ~205 Ma, e.g. Morganucodon), earliest artiodactyls (early Eocene, ~55 Ma, e.g. Diacodexis) — see Benton, M.J. (2015) Vertebrate Palaeontology, 4th ed., Wiley-Blackwell, and Clack (2012). Whale dates as on the following slides (Uhen 2010). Middle ear: the mammalian malleus and incus derive from jaw bones of earlier amniotes; in whales the ear bones are isolated in a dense bulla for underwater hearing (Nummela et al. 2004).

Transition 1 · a wading relative

Indohyus: the raoellids, sister group of whales.

  • Raccoon-sized artiodactyl from the middle Eocene of Kashmir, India (~48 Ma), described from a skeleton in 2007.
  • Shares with whales the involucrum: a thickened wall of the auditory bulla otherwise found only in cetaceans.
  • Heavy, osteosclerotic limb bones (ballast, as in hippos and otters) and oxygen isotopes of teeth indicate time spent in water.
  • Teeth suggest a plant-based or omnivorous diet — the first step into water was not about hunting fish.

Why it matters. Indohyus shows that aquatic habits (wading, hiding in water) preceded the dietary and locomotor changes seen in true whales; the ear came first.

Thewissen, J.G.M., Cooper, L.N., Clementz, M.T., Bajpai, S. & Tiwari, B.N. (2007) "Whales originated from aquatic artiodactyls in the Eocene epoch of India", Nature 450:1190–1194.
Transition 2 · the earliest whales

Pakicetus (~50 Ma): a whale that walked on four legs.

  • Early Eocene of Pakistan (Kuldana Formation); first described from a skull in 1983, skeletons in 2001.
  • Wolf-sized, long-snouted, with eyes set high on the skull; limbs built for land and shallow water — not a swimmer's body.
  • Recognised as a cetacean by its ear (involucrum, pachyosteosclerotic bulla) and teeth, long before its body was known.
  • Ankle bones with the artiodactyl double-pulley astragalus: the fossil and molecular stories agree.

Transition 3 · the walking, swimming whale

Ambulocetus natans (~49 Ma): an ambush predator at the water's edge.

  • About 3 m long, from the early–middle Eocene of Pakistan; large hind feet and a powerful tail suggest swimming by pelvic paddling and undulation, like an otter.
  • Could still support itself on land; crocodile-like ecology inferred from skull shape and stable isotopes (fresh to brackish water).
  • Hearing: early whales had a mandibular fat pad pathway for underwater sound, while pakicetids still heard mostly in air (Nummela et al. 2004).

Naming. Ambulocetus natans = "the walking whale that swims" — the name says exactly what the skeleton shows.

Thewissen, J.G.M., Hussain, S.T. & Arif, M. (1994) "Fossil evidence for the origin of aquatic locomotion in archaeocete whales", Science 263:210–212. Nummela, S., Thewissen, J.G.M., Bajpai, S., Hussain, S.T. & Kumar, K. (2004) "Eocene evolution of whale hearing", Nature 430:776–778.
Transition 4 · going to sea

Protocetids: seagoing whales that still gave birth on land.

  • Rodhocetus (~47 Ma, Pakistan): shortened neck, robust tail, hind limbs still weight-bearing (Gingerich et al. 1994).
  • Maiacetus inuus (~47.5 Ma): a female preserved with a near-term fetus positioned head-first — modern whales are born tail-first — implying birth on land (Gingerich et al. 2009).
  • Peregocetus pacificus (~42.6 Ma, Peru): a four-legged whale on the Pacific coast of South America, showing protocetids had crossed the Atlantic by the middle Eocene (Lambert et al. 2019).

Transition 5 · fully aquatic

Basilosaurids (~41–34 Ma): whales that could no longer leave the water.

  • Basilosaurus (up to ~17–18 m, eel-like body) and Dorudon (~5 m): fully marine, with tail flukes and flipper-like forelimbs.
  • Tiny but complete hind limbs preserved in Basilosaurus isis — with knee, ankle and toes — too small to bear weight (Gingerich et al. 1990).
  • Nostrils have moved back along the snout; the pelvis is no longer attached to the spine.
  • Best known from Wadi Al-Hitan ("Valley of Whales"), Egypt — a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2005.

The hind limb (small triangle near the tail) is a few percent of body length.

Transition 6 · the great split

Baleen whales began with teeth.

  • Around 36–34 Ma the crown group Neoceti splits into Mysticeti (baleen whales) and Odontoceti (toothed whales).
  • Mystacodon selenensis (36.4 Ma, Peru) is the oldest known mysticete — and it has teeth, with wear suggesting suction feeding (Lambert et al. 2017).
  • Coronodon havensteini (Oligocene, South Carolina) apparently filtered with its teeth, using slots between them (Geisler et al. 2017).
  • Aetiocetids had teeth plus, in some interpretations, incipient baleen; Llanocetus (~34 Ma, Antarctica) was already toothed and large (Fordyce & Marx 2018).
  • Baleen itself rarely fossilises; its presence is inferred from nutrient foramina on the palate.

Transition 7 · seeing with sound

Toothed whales evolved echolocation early.

  • Cotylocara macei (~28 Ma, South Carolina) shows a skull with asymmetry and deep air sinuses — the bony correlates of a sound-producing nasal apparatus (Geisler et al. 2014).
  • Echovenator sandersi (Oligocene) had a cochlea adapted to ultrasonic hearing, indicating high-frequency hearing arose at or near the base of Odontoceti (Churchill et al. 2016).
  • Modern toothed whales produce clicks with phonic lips in the nasal passages, focus them through the melon, and receive echoes through the fat-filled lower jaw.

Two strategies, one ancestor. Within a few million years of the Neoceti split, one lineage was screening prey out of water, the other was ranging it with sound. Both are solutions to feeding in an ocean where the eyes are of limited use.

Geisler, J.H., Colbert, M.W. & Carew, J.L. (2014) "A new fossil species supports an early origin for toothed whale echolocation", Nature 508:383–386. Churchill, M., Martínez-Cáceres, M., de Muizon, C., Mnieckowski, J. & Geisler, J.H. (2016) "The origin of high-frequency hearing in whales", Current Biology 26:2144–2149.
Transition 8 · becoming enormous

Whales were not always giants. Gigantism is geologically recent.

  • Body-size analysis of living and fossil baleen whales shows very large size (> 10 m) evolving independently in several lineages within the last ~4.5 million years (Slater et al. 2017).
  • The proposed driver: Plio-Pleistocene cooling and wind-driven coastal upwelling concentrated prey into dense seasonal patches, rewarding lunge-feeders that travel far and eat in bulk.
  • The blue whale, up to ~30 m and well over 100 tonnes, is the largest animal known to have lived; energetic measurements suggest filter-feeding sizes are capped by the cost of a lunge (Goldbogen et al. 2019).
Slater, G.J., Goldbogen, J.A. & Pyenson, N.D. (2017) "Independent evolution of baleen whale gigantism linked to Plio-Pleistocene ocean dynamics", Proceedings of the Royal Society B 284:20170546. Goldbogen, J.A. et al. (2019) "Why whales are big but not bigger: physiological drivers and ecological limits in the age of ocean giants", Science 366:1367–1372. Body lengths: Uhen (2004) for Dorudon; Gingerich (1990s) for Basilosaurus (~16–18 m); Thewissen et al. (2001) for Pakicetus (wolf-sized).
Transition 9 · written in the genome

The land-to-water transition left a trail of lost genes.

  • Comparing 62 mammalian genomes, Huelsmann et al. (2019) identified 85 protein-coding genes lost in the cetacean stem lineage.
  • Examples with plausible aquatic explanations: AANAT and ASMT (melatonin synthesis — consistent with unihemispheric sleep), F12 (a coagulation factor; a possible protection against clotting during dives), KLK8 (skin and hair function), SLC4A9 (saliva secretion).
  • A genome-scale phylogeny of living cetaceans (McGowen et al. 2020) dates the crown Neoceti divergence and resolves the placement of most families.

Caveat. Gene loss correlates with the transition; the functional stories are hypotheses tested one gene at a time, and not all losses have known consequences.

(2020) "Phylogenomic resolution of the cetacean tree of life using target sequence capture", Systematic Biology 69:479–501.

Not only whales · what the returns have in common

Different ancestors, same solutions — and one whale-specific twist.

Convergences

  • Streamlined body, tail propulsion: ichthyosaurs, whales and tuna converged on the same outline; ichthyosaurs beat sideways like fish, whales up and down like running mammals.
  • Limbs → flippers, often with extra finger bones (hyperphalangy) in ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs and whales.
  • Dense bones first, light bones later: early stages of many lineages show ballast-like thickened bone (whales, sea cows, early ichthyosaurs), replaced by lighter skeletons once they become fast open-water swimmers (Houssaye 2009).
  • Live birth: ichthyosaurs gave birth tail-first at sea, like whales — and, judging by the earliest embryos, had acquired viviparity on land beforehand (Motani et al. 2014).

Where whales differ

  • Most returning lineages keep a foothold on land — seals, turtles and penguins still come ashore to breed. Whales and sea cows are among the few that never leave the water.
  • Only toothed whales evolved echolocation; only baleen whales evolved bulk filter feeding at giant size.
  • No known lineage has gone water → land → water → land again: once fully marine, the return ticket seems lost.
Kelley & Pyenson (2015) on convergence and ecological roles of marine tetrapods. Motani, R., Jiang, D.-Y., Tintori, A., Rieppel, O. & Chen, G.-B. (2014) "Terrestrial origin of viviparity in Mesozoic marine reptiles indicated by Early Triassic embryonic fossils", PLoS ONE 9:e88640. Houssaye, A. The statement that no lineage has returned to land after becoming fully marine reflects the fossil record as reviewed by Vermeij & Motani (2018); amphibious lineages (e.g. seals) are not counted as "fully marine".

Ear first, then feet, then sound and size.

Fish left the water once, ~375 million years ago; their descendants have gone back dozens of times. Whales are the lineage that went furthest, and the best documented: molecules, fossils and development all tell the same story, from Ellesmere Island to Kashmir to Peru.
